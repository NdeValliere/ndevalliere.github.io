Three ways to put grain on a blurred image, plus your two files

Panels 3–5 are all built from panel 1 — the clean PNG — the same way: blurred at σ1.2, then re-encoded to AVIF at crf 18. The only variable between them is how the grain arrives. Panel 1 is your clean plate; panel 2 is your Photoshop version of it, for comparison.

Grain measured as the standard deviation of each image minus its own σ1.5 blur, averaged over the twelve flattest 48×48 patches in the source (structure std 0.25–0.44), at the size each panel is actually displayed. Measuring whole-image instead lets the object's edges dominate and makes every number meaningless — the clean PNG scores 3.40 that way.

The headline: grain is high-frequency and random, so it defeats every prediction a codec wants to make. That is why baking it in is a losing trade: panel 3 spends 2.3× the bytes of panel 4 and still ends up with a fifth of the grain, because the encoder burns its bitrate on noise and then smooths it away regardless. If the grain has to live inside the image, let the format model it (4). If it can sit on top, draw it (5) — the blur itself is nearly free, so the grain is the entire file size.

Caveat on panel 5: feTurbulence at these sub-pixel base frequencies is rendered at slightly different scales by Safari, Chrome and Firefox, so its grain is not identical between them — though it does regenerate at device resolution, so it stays crisp on any display. To pin it down, rasterise the noise once to a tiling PNG: 128×128 is 14.4 KB at 8-bit but only 6.1 KB at 4-bit for the same standard deviation (26.3 vs 25.8) — random noise is near-incompressible, so bit depth, not dimensions, is where the savings are. Expect to dial it to roughly 30% opacity under color-burn: an opaque tile blends far harder than feTurbulence, whose output carries a random alpha channel.
